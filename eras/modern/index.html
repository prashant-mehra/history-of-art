Modern art

~1900–1970

Modern artists questioned what art should be: representation gave way to color, form, idea and gesture.

Early modernism

Fauvism's wild color, Expressionism's emotion, and Cubism's fragmented view of objects broke up traditional space.

Abstraction

Kandinsky, Malevich and Mondrian pursued art free of recognizable subjects.

Dada and Surrealism

Dada mocked war-era values and Duchamp proposed the ready-made. Surrealism explored dreams and the unconscious.

Mexico and the Americas

Kahlo, Rivera, Orozco and Siqueiros built a politically engaged art. The Harlem Renaissance produced Jacob Lawrence and Aaron Douglas.

Postwar

Abstract Expressionism in New York shifted the art world's center from Paris. Pop Art in the 1960s brought in advertising and consumer culture.

Architecture

The Bauhaus, Le Corbusier and Frank Lloyd Wright created architecture of steel, glass and concrete.

Movements and styles

~1905–1908

Fauvism

Bold, non-naturalistic color. 'Fauves' means wild beasts.

~1905–1930s

Expressionism

Distorting form and color to express inner feeling. Groups such as Die Brücke and Der Blaue Reiter.

~1907–1920s

Cubism

Objects broken into geometric planes and shown from many viewpoints at once.

~1915–1920s

Suprematism

Malevich's art of basic geometric forms, as 'the supremacy of pure feeling'.

1917–1931

De Stijl

Dutch movement reducing art and design to straight lines and primary colors.

~1916–1924

Dada

An anti-art protest born in the First World War; it mocked reason, nationalism and tradition.

~1924–1950s

Surrealism

Exploring dreams, the unconscious and chance. Artists like Dalí, Magritte, Ernst and Kahlo.

~1900–1970

Modernist architecture

Function, new materials and clear forms replaced historical ornament.

~1940s–1960

Abstract Expressionism

Large abstract canvases that express emotion through gesture or fields of color. Pollock, Rothko, de Kooning.

~1950s–1970s

Pop Art

Borrowing from advertising, comics and mass media, using irony and repetition.
