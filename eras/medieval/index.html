Medieval art

~300–1400 CE

The Middle Ages brought sacred art across Europe, Byzantium and the Islamic world. Images taught the faith, glorified rulers or decorated holy buildings.

Early Christian and Byzantine (~300–1453)

Gold-ground mosaics and icons stressed spiritual presence over realism. The Hagia Sophia in Constantinople (537) is the great monument.

Insular and early medieval

Monks in Ireland and Britain created intricate manuscripts such as the Book of Kells.

Romanesque and Gothic (~1000–1400)

Romanesque churches have thick walls and rounded arches. Gothic cathedrals use pointed arches, rib vaults and flying buttresses to rise high and fill with stained glass.

Islamic art

Calligraphy, geometric pattern and arabesque decorate mosques, palaces, books and metalwork, from the Dome of the Rock to the Alhambra.

Beyond Europe

Contemporary traditions flourished in Tang and Song China, in Heian Japan, in West Africa and in the Maya lowlands. See the regions pages.

Movements and styles

~330–1453

Byzantine art

The art of the eastern Roman Empire, centered on Constantinople. Gold mosaics and icons present sacred figures as timeless.

7th century onward

Islamic art

Art of the Islamic world, favoring calligraphy, geometry and arabesque over figures in religious settings.

~1000–1150

Romanesque

Solid stone churches with round arches and carved portals; heavy walls with small windows.

~1140–1500

Gothic

Pointed arches, ribbed vaults and flying buttresses allowed tall, light-filled cathedrals with stained glass.
