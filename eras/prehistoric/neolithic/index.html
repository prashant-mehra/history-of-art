Neolithic art

~9500 – 2000 BCE (varies by region) · Worldwide

Farming and herding led to villages, stored surplus, and new materials. Art moved into the home and the landscape.

Pottery

Fired clay vessels appear in East Asia as early as ~14,000 years ago (Jōmon pottery in Japan, later known for dramatic cord-impressed surfaces). Elsewhere, pots were painted, incised or burnished: Linear Pottery culture in Europe, Cucuteni–Trypillia painted wares, and Yangshao painted pottery in China.

Art in the house

  • Çatalhöyük, Türkiye (~7100–5700 BCE). Densely packed mud-brick houses with plastered, painted walls and sculpted bull heads.
  • Ain Ghazal, Jordan (~7200–6500 BCE). Life-sized plaster statues with painted details and bitumen-ringed eyes.
  • Jericho (~7000 BCE). Skulls modelled with plaster and shell eyes, evidence of ancestor veneration.
  • Skara Brae, Orkney (~3180 BCE). Stone houses with built-in furniture, and carved patterned stones.

Megaliths

"Megalith" means "great stone." Communities across Europe moved enormous stones to build tombs, circles and alignments.

  • Newgrange, Ireland (~3200 BCE). A passage tomb decorated with spirals and lozenges; at the winter solstice sunrise a shaft of light enters the chamber.
  • Stonehenge, England. Built in stages from about 3000 BCE; the great sarsen stones were raised around 2500 BCE.
  • Carnac, France. Thousands of standing stones in long rows.
Neolithic art is the bridge to the next chapter: as cities and writing emerge in Mesopotamia and Egypt, art becomes tied to kings and gods.
