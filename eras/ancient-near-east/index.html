Ancient Near East

~3500–330 BCE

The Near East (Mesopotamia, Persia and neighbors) is the home of the first cities and the first writing. Its art served kings and gods: monumental reliefs, temple statuary, seals and glazed brick.

Sumer and Akkad (~3500–2100 BCE)

City-states such as Ur built ziggurats (stepped temple towers). Sumerian art includes small votive statues with huge, wide-open eyes, and cylinder seals that were rolled over clay to leave a repeating scene.

Babylon and Assyria (~1900–539 BCE)

Babylonian kings set up law and victory monuments, and Assyrian palaces were lined with carved stone reliefs of hunts and battles. Under Nebuchadnezzar II, Babylon's gates were covered in glazed brick.

Persia (~550–330 BCE)

The Achaemenid Empire created the palace at Persepolis, with processions of subject peoples carved on its stairways, expressing an empire that included many cultures.

Why it matters

Many ideas appear here first: writing as decoration, narrative relief carving, and art used to project royal power.
