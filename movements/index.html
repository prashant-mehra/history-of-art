Movements and styles

Art is often grouped into movements: shared aims, styles or ideas. The groupings are a convenience, and many artists crossed them.

~480 BCE–5th century CE

Classical art

The art of ancient Greece and Rome, built on idealized proportion, balance and the human body.

~330–1453

Byzantine art

The art of the eastern Roman Empire, centered on Constantinople. Gold mosaics and icons present sacred figures as timeless.

7th century onward

Islamic art

Art of the Islamic world, favoring calligraphy, geometry and arabesque over figures in religious settings.

~1000–1150

Romanesque

Solid stone churches with round arches and carved portals; heavy walls with small windows.

~1140–1500

Gothic

Pointed arches, ribbed vaults and flying buttresses allowed tall, light-filled cathedrals with stained glass.

~1400–1600

Renaissance

A 'rebirth' of classical ideals, linear perspective and close observation of nature.

~1600–1750

Baroque

Drama, movement and strong contrasts of light, used to stir the emotions.

~1730–1780

Rococo

A lighter, ornamental and playful style of pastel colors and curving forms.

~1760–1830

Neoclassicism

A return to the clarity, order and moral seriousness of antiquity, boosted by the discoveries at Pompeii.

~1800–1850

Romanticism

Emotion, imagination and the awe of nature over reason and order.

~1840s–1880s

Realism

Depicting ordinary people and daily life honestly, without idealization. Also used for later works of everyday modern life.

~1650–1900

Ukiyo-e

Japanese woodblock prints of 'the floating world': actors, landscapes, beauties. A major influence on European artists.

~1870–1886

Impressionism

Painting the effect of light and atmosphere with short, visible brushstrokes, often outdoors.

~1885–1905

Post-Impressionism

Artists who built on Impressionism but sought more structure, emotion or symbol.

~1905–1908

Fauvism

Bold, non-naturalistic color. 'Fauves' means wild beasts.

~1905–1930s

Expressionism

Distorting form and color to express inner feeling. Groups such as Die Brücke and Der Blaue Reiter.

~1907–1920s

Cubism

Objects broken into geometric planes and shown from many viewpoints at once.

~1915–1920s

Suprematism

Malevich's art of basic geometric forms, as 'the supremacy of pure feeling'.

1917–1931

De Stijl

Dutch movement reducing art and design to straight lines and primary colors.

~1916–1924

Dada

An anti-art protest born in the First World War; it mocked reason, nationalism and tradition.

~1924–1950s

Surrealism

Exploring dreams, the unconscious and chance. Artists like Dalí, Magritte, Ernst and Kahlo.

~1900–1970

Modernist architecture

Function, new materials and clear forms replaced historical ornament.

~1940s–1960

Abstract Expressionism

Large abstract canvases that express emotion through gesture or fields of color. Pollock, Rothko, de Kooning.

~1950s–1970s

Pop Art

Borrowing from advertising, comics and mass media, using irony and repetition.

~1960s–1970s

Minimalism

Stripped-down geometric forms and industrial materials. Associated with Donald Judd and Agnes Martin. Kusama's early work is often discussed alongside it.

~1960s onward

Conceptual art

The idea is more important than the finished object. Includes text pieces, performance and installations.

~1970s onward

Street art and graffiti

Art made in public space without permission, or in dialogue with it, from tags and murals to stencils.

~1960s onward

Digital art

Art made with or about computers: algorithmic, networked, generative and interactive work.
